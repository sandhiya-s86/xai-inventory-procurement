# Explainable AI-Driven Decision Support for Predictive Inventory and Procurement Optimization in ERP Systems

**PBL-1 Report · Machine Learning (Units 1 & 2)**

Every numerical claim in this report is produced by code in this repository
and stored in `reports/`. Nothing is estimated or recalled from memory.
Reproduce with `python scripts/run_pipeline.py`.

---

## 1. Abstract

Retail inventory management is a two-sided failure problem: stockouts lose
revenue, overstock immobilises capital. This project builds an end-to-end
decision-support system that predicts short-horizon demand, classifies
stockout risk, converts both into a constrained purchase recommendation, and
explains the recommendation in terms a buyer can audit.

Using 1,067,371 real transactions from UCI Online Retail II, seven regression
models and six classification models were trained with strict chronological
validation. **Ridge regression (α = 100)** gave the best 7-day demand forecast
(test RMSE 288.53, R² 0.510), and **LightGBM** the best reorder classifier
(F1 0.778, ROC-AUC 0.921). LightGBM quantile models supplied calibrated
uncertainty (P90 coverage 89.2% against a 90% target).

The central finding is negative and informative: **a better point forecast
alone does not improve service.** A policy using the ML forecast without a
safety buffer achieved a 79.2% fill rate, *below* the 79.9% naive baseline.
Service improved only when uncertainty was modelled explicitly — the full
policy reached **93.9% fill rate**, a 14.0 percentage-point gain with stockout
days reduced 75% (2,027 → 496), at 4.6% higher simulated cost.

Explanations are exact rather than indicative: Shapley values are computed in
closed form for the linear forecaster, and the recommended quantity is
decomposed into named components that sum to the order with zero residual
across all 16,689 decisions.

## 2. Introduction

A purchasing decision requires three things: an expectation of future demand,
a position on acceptable risk, and the supplier constraints that bound the
order. Most ML coursework supplies only the first. This project deliberately
carries all three through to a number a buyer could act on, and then insists
that number be explainable.

The "in ERP Systems" framing is a design constraint, not a deployment claim.
The system is built against an ERP-shaped entity model (Item, Stock Ledger,
Supplier, Purchase Order) so that integration would be a mapping exercise. No
ERP is connected; see §23 and `docs/erp_integration_ready.md`.

---

## 3. Problem Statement

Given historical sales for a SKU, an inventory position, and supplier terms,
answer six questions at any decision date:

1. How much demand is expected over the next 7 days?
2. Is current stock sufficient?
3. Is there stockout risk before replenishment can arrive?
4. Should the item be reordered?
5. How much should be ordered?
6. **Why?**

Questions 1 and 5 are regression; 3 and 4 are classification; 6 is the part
that determines whether the other five get used.

**Retained: 1,024,819 of 1,067,371 (96.01%).**

Two preprocessing decisions deserve emphasis:

**Zeros are not imputed blindly.** A zero is written only where the business
traded and the SKU did not sell. The 135 non-trading days are excluded from
the calendar entirely.

**Prices are carried forward, not zero-filled.** An unsold item still has a
price; zero would corrupt the simulated cost basis.

## 9. Feature Engineering

43 regression features, 52 classification features.

**Timing convention.** A row `(sku, t)` is a decision at the *start* of day t.
Features use `t−1` and earlier; the target covers `t … t+6`. Every rolling
statistic is computed on a `.shift(1)` series with `min_periods = window`, so
partial windows become NaN and are dropped rather than silently
under-computed.

| Group | Count | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Calendar | 14 | `day_of_week`, `is_q4_peak`, `days_to_bank_holiday` |
| Lag | 4 | `lag_1`, `lag_7`, `lag_14`, `lag_28` |
| Rolling | 15 | `rolling_mean_28`, `rolling_std_7`, `zero_rate_28` |
| Trend | 3 | `demand_momentum`, `demand_cv_28` |
| Price (proxy) | 4 | `price_discount_pct`, `is_price_cut` |
| Returns | 1 | `returns_mean_28` |
| SKU | 2 | `sku_rank`, `median_price` |
| Inventory (classification only) | 9 | `current_stock`, `days_of_cover`, `lead_time_days` |

The forecaster deliberately excludes stock level: how much is on the shelf does
not change customer demand, and including it would model the replenishment
policy rather than demand itself.

**Leakage control is tested, not asserted.** `tests/test_no_leakage.py` runs a
strictly increasing synthetic series where any leak is arithmetically visible,
verifies `rolling_max_7` equals exactly yesterday's value, confirms the target
formula on a known sequence, and scans every real feature for correlation
above 0.97 with the target.

| Model | Unit | MAE | RMSE | R² | RMSLE | Train (s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ridge Regression (L2)** | U2 | 136.36 | **288.53** | **0.510** | 1.133 | 0.17 |
| Bayesian Linear Regression | U2 | 136.32 | 288.67 | 0.509 | 1.130 | 0.92 |
| Linear Regression (OLS) | U1+U2 | 134.19 | 288.96 | 0.508 | 1.115 | 0.28 |
| Robust (Huber) | U2 | **124.04** | 297.38 | 0.479 | **0.939** | 26.77 |
| *Baseline: 28-day MA × h* | — | 136.70 | 298.13 | 0.477 | 0.991 | 0.00 |
| LightGBM | — | 142.28 | 311.43 | 0.429 | 1.178 | 2.22 |
| *Baseline: 7-day MA × h* | — | 160.32 | 352.71 | 0.267 | 1.053 | 0.00 |

**Selected: Ridge, α = 100.** Chosen on validation from six candidates
(`ridge_alpha_search.csv`); the test set played no part in that choice.

Three results reported honestly:

1. **LightGBM placed 6th of 7**, behind a moving average, even with early
   stopping (halted at 36 trees, improving RMSE from 342.93 to 311.43). Causes:
   31.7% zero-inflation fragments tree splits; the Q4 test period exceeds the
   training range and trees cannot extrapolate; rolling features already encode
   the interactions a tree would need to find.
2. **Huber wins MAE and RMSLE but loses RMSE** — exactly what a robust loss
   does. If the business cared about typical-day accuracy, Huber would be the
   correct choice. RMSE is the declared primary metric because inventory cost
   is driven by large misses.
3. **The margin over the naive baseline is modest** — 3.2% RMSE improvement.

Positive rate 30.8%, so a constant "no" scores 69.2% accuracy while catching

drive selection because a missed stockout costs more than a surplus order.

**Generative vs discriminative, measured.** Naive Bayes reaches 0.969 recall
but 0.374 precision — its independence assumption is violated by correlated
rolling means, making it over-confident. Logistic regression models `p(y|x)`
directly and is far better calibrated (F1 0.748 vs 0.540).

**Kernel trick, measured.** Linear SVM (0.748) beats RBF SVM (0.718) with
identical C, features and subsample. The infinite-dimensional map did not help:
the boundary is close to linear in these features.

## 15. Inventory Simulation (SIMULATED)

A transparent single-echelon simulator drives the stock trajectory using a
deliberately plain weekly-review policy on a trailing demand mean. It never
looks forward. Its weakness is the point: it is the "current practice"
baseline, producing an 80.1% fill rate with unmet demand on 10.1% of SKU-days,
and 28.3% of labelled rows flagged as needing a reorder.

Conservation is enforced and tested:

```
ending_stock = beginning_stock + arrivals − consumed
consumed     = min(demand, beginning_stock + arrivals)
```

Maximum residual across all 120,800 SKU-days: **0.000000000000**.

## 16. Procurement Decision Logic

```
raw_need = expected_lead_time_demand + safety_stock − inventory_position
qty      = max(0, raw_need)
qty      = max(qty, MOQ)                     if qty > 0
qty      = ceil(qty / pack_size) × pack_size
qty      = min(qty, floor(capacity / pack) × pack)
```

The capacity cap rounds **down** to a whole pack — a part pack cannot be
bought, and clipping straight to the capacity figure silently breaks the
pack-multiple guarantee. This was caught by
`tests/test_reorder_policy.py::test_real_recommendations_obey_every_constraint`
during development, not by inspection.

Output: 16,689 decisions, 91.5% recommending a reorder, mean order 616 units.

## 17. Policy Comparison and Ablation

| Policy | Fill rate | Stockout days | Orders | Mean inv. | Shortage cost | Total cost | vs P0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P0 moving average | 79.9% | 2,027 | 1,183 | 474 | 386,250 | 1,086,570 | — |
| P1 classical reorder point | 88.7% | 966 | 1,670 | 586 | 232,204 | 1,054,847 | **−2.9%** |
| P2 ML forecast, no buffer | 79.2% | 1,570 | 1,788 | 318 | 368,161 | 1,077,805 | −0.8% |
| P3 ML forecast + classical buffer | 89.7% | 767 | 1,678 | 590 | 201,178 | 1,069,475 | −1.6% |
| **P4 ML + quantile buffer** | **93.9%** | **496** | 1,667 | 793 | **125,062** | 1,136,500 | +4.6% |

**The central result.** P2 — a better point forecast with no safety buffer —
achieves a fill rate of 79.2%, **0.7 points below the naive baseline**. Forecast
accuracy alone did not help. What helped was modelling uncertainty: P1 gains
8.8 points using a classical buffer and no ML at all; P4 gains 14.0 points
using the learned quantile spread, cutting stockout days 75% and shortage cost
68%.

**P1 is cost-minimal; P4 maximises service.** Which is "best" depends on the
shortage-cost multiplier (currently 2.5), a parameter placed in
`sim/supply_config.yaml` precisely so it can be challenged. In giftware
wholesale a stockout also risks losing the retailer's next order, which the
simulator does not model and therefore understates.

## 18. Explainable AI

**Two layers, because only one of them answers the buyer's question.**

*Why this forecast?* — SHAP attributions. The deployed forecaster is Ridge, a
linear model, so Shapley values have the closed form
`φᵢ = βᵢ(zᵢ − E[zᵢ])` and are **exact**. Additivity verified: max
|base + Σφ − prediction| = **6.1 × 10⁻⁴**. Top drivers: `rolling_mean_28`
(93.7), `rolling_std_14` (69.6), `rolling_mean_7` (55.7).

For the classifier, TreeSHAP in log-odds space; additivity error
**8.5 × 10⁻¹³**. Top drivers: `stock_vs_leadtime_demand` (1.87),
`current_stock` (0.50), `days_of_cover` (0.40). Note the two rankings are
almost disjoint — demand is driven by demand history, but the *decision* is
driven by inventory position, exactly as a buyer would expect.

*Why this quantity?* — an exact arithmetic decomposition. Worked example
(SKU 20727, test period):

```
Stock + on order       : 347.2 units
Predicted 7-day demand : 486.3 units
Lead time              : 8 days
Stockout risk          : HIGH  (p = 0.9999)

  +1,042.11   expected demand over lead time + review period
    +674.98   safety stock (learned P95 − P50 spread)
    -347.20   less: stock already available / on order
      +0.00   clip at zero
      +0.00   supplier minimum order quantity
     +10.11   pack-size rounding
      +0.00   supplier capacity cap
  ──────────
      1,380   FINAL ORDER QUANTITY
```

Maximum decomposition residual across all 16,689 decisions: **0.000000**.
`tests/test_decision_explanation.py` includes a test that tampers with a
component and asserts the explainer *refuses to render* rather than printing a
wrong explanation.

## 22. Business Interpretation

**Buy uncertainty, not accuracy.** The project's own ablation shows a better
point forecast delivering nothing (P2: −0.7pp fill rate) while an explicit
safety buffer delivers +8.8 to +14.0pp. For a retailer with a limited
analytics budget, implementing *any* principled safety stock beats refining
the forecast.

**The service/cost trade is a management decision, not a modelling one.** P4
buys 14 points of fill rate for 4.6% more cost. Whether that is correct depends
on the price of a lost sale. The system surfaces the trade rather than making
it.

**Explanation is the adoption mechanism.** A buyer who cannot see why the
system asked for 1,380 units will override it. The decomposition makes review
fast enough to actually happen, and the what-if panel lets the buyer test their
own intuition against the model.

## 23. Limitations

1. **Supply-side data is simulated.** The largest caveat. Stock, suppliers,
   lead times, MOQ and all costs are synthetic. Policy *rankings* are valid;
   currency amounts are not claims about the real retailer.
2. **One retailer, one country, two years.** No evidence of transfer.
3. **Top 200 SKUs only** — 42.0% of cleaned revenue. The long tail, where
   intermittency is most extreme, is unmodelled.
4. **No promotion data.** `price_discount_pct` is a proxy, not a promotion flag.
5. **Cost parameters are assumptions.** The shortage multiplier of 2.5
   materially determines whether P1 or P4 is "best".
6. **Modest gains over a naive baseline** — 3.2% RMSE.
7. **Classifier recall 0.816** — roughly 18% of genuine reorder situations are
   missed, which is why human review is mandatory.
8. **No ERP integration.** Readiness is designed for and documented; nothing is
   connected.
9. **Single echelon, single warehouse.**

## 25. Conclusion

This project converts 1,067,371 real retail transactions into auditable
purchasing decisions, implementing every Unit 1 and Unit 2 topic in code that
runs, with each topic mapped to its source file and output artefact.

Two findings are worth more than the headline metrics, and both are negative.
**A better point forecast did not improve service** — the gain came from
quantifying uncertainty. And **the most sophisticated model did not win the
forecasting task**: LightGBM placed sixth of seven, behind a moving average,
because zero-inflated demand and a test period outside the training range suit
a regularised linear model better. Both are reported rather than hidden,
because a project that only reports what worked has not actually tested
anything.

The explanation layer is where the engineering effort concentrated. Shapley
values are exact rather than approximated, and the recommended quantity is
decomposed into components that sum to the order with zero residual across all
16,689 decisions — enforced by a test that fails if the identity ever breaks.
That is the difference between a system that is explainable and one that merely
produces explanations.
